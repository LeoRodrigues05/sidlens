How the SID recommenders use history

Intervention experiments, a timestamp audit and two activation studies on the Qwen AR and DiffGRM recommenders. In both paradigms, most of the next item comes from copying: the most recent item sets the first digit, and each later digit is read from a history item that already matches the prefix. The models copy by position in the list, while in the data it is an item from the same day that predicts the next one.

Recency. Replacing the user's most recent item costs the correct first SID digit about half a nat in both paradigms: 0.57 and 0.51 nats for the AR models, and 0.56 and 0.48 for DiffGRM. The AR model's top first digit changes in 75% and 50% of rows.

Copying. Later digits are copied from whichever history item already shares the prefix generated so far. We call this prefix-matched copying. The AR models read that item's next-digit token directly, through attention in layers 14 to 27, spread over many heads. DiffGRM spreads the item through its encoder into the other history slots and reads it there.

Effect on recommendations. Blocking the copy reads inside the archived beam-search decoder lowers HR@10 for RQ-KMeans (−5.7 pp [−6.8, −4.6]) and lowers it for RQ-VAE (−3.3 pp [−4.1, −2.5]). On repeat targets, the SID the user already had, the change is −27.3 pp [−32.3, −22.1] and −10.1 pp [−14.9, −5.8]; on new targets, −2.1 pp [−3.1, −1.2] and −3.0 pp [−3.8, −2.1].

Time. No model sees a timestamp. In the data, the item that predicts the target is one from the same day, wherever it sits in the list; the models copy whatever sits last. RQ-KMeans AR copies the most recent item's first digit in 67% of same-day rows and 67% of rows where it is over a year old, against 33% and 3% that the data justify. Same-day rows carry 87% and 88% of the copy benefit, and duplicate review records supply 11% and 17% of all HR@10 hits.

Features. Sparse autoencoders find code-specific copy features that are causal from layer 20, and prefix-match detectors that the output does not read at the readout. They find no target information beyond the history, but a same-day burst signal the copy ignores.

1 · Setup

What was run

Every experiment follows the same pattern. First, a protocol states the question, conditions, estimands and expectations before any outcome exists. Next come controls that must reproduce the unintervened model bit for bit. Last, a paired bootstrap over users gives the intervals. Effects are reported as Δ = score(clean) − score(intervened) in nats, so a positive Δ means the intervention hurt the correct answer. Scores are the correct code's log-probability among that digit's codes, taken where the model predicts the digit. exp6 is the exception: it measures hit rate in real beam-search decoding.

2 · Recency · exp3, exp7

The most recent item carries the first digit

Replacing the most recent item with a catalogue item that has a different first digit costs the correct first digit about half a nat. The second most recent item matters a quarter to a half as much, and items further back barely matter. Keeping the replacement's first digit removes most of the effect: 82% and 64% of it for the AR models, 73% and 63% for DiffGRM. That first digit is where the evidence sits.

3 · Mechanism, AR · exp3, exp4, probes

How the AR models copy

Patching shows where the evidence lives. It stays on the replaced item's own tokens until about layer 20. Then, over layers 19 to 24, it moves to the position that predicts the first digit. Later digits follow the same path to the target tokens.

An unexpected pattern in exp3 became the hypothesis for exp4. Changing a history item's later digits hurt the matching output digit, but only when that item already shared the prefix generated so far. exp4 tested this on the valid split, which played no part in forming the hypothesis. The pattern replicated. Blocking a single attention link, from the position that predicts digit d to digit d of the matching item, removes about a third of the effect. The same link aimed at a non-matching item does nothing. The read happens only in layers 14 to 27.

The captured activations show the same thing from the inside. The model's own first-digit choice is the recent item's first digit in most rows, and that preference switches on late. Nothing a linear probe can read at that position predicts the correct first digit better than copying does.

4 · Recommendation quality · exp6

Does copying help recommendations?

This experiment asks the question that matters for the paper. It re-runs the archived AR decoder (trie-constrained, 50 beams, reproduced batch for batch) with the copy reads blocked in layers 14 to 27. At each decoding step, each beam loses its reads of digit d from history items that match that beam's own prefix; for the first digit, reads of every history item. Targets are split by whether their SID already appears in the user's history (repeat, 521 rows, 14.2%) or not (new).

Copying helps, and most of all on repeats. Repeat targets are 14% (RQ-KMeans) and 5% (RQ-VAE) of rows, but they supply 54% and 31% of the baseline's HR@10 hits. No block raises new-target HR@10 beyond noise. Blocking only the later-digit reads costs RQ-VAE's new targets −2.8 pp [−3.6, −2.0], so copying a matching item's next digit also finds new items that share a prefix with the history; for RQ-KMeans the same block gives +0.1 pp [−0.3, +0.4]. The control, blocking as many non-matching reads, moves HR@10 by −0.03 pp [−0.19, +0.13] and −0.11 pp [−0.31, +0.08]. The knockout reaches the output: the share of top-1 SIDs already in the history falls from 58.4% to 18.4% and from 25.1% to 9.5%. Under plain beam search, the same block changes repeat-target HR@10 by −19.4 pp [−23.7, −15.6] and −8.4 pp [−12.8, −4.5], and new-target HR@10 by −1.5 pp [−2.3, −0.6] and −3.0 pp [−3.7, −2.2]. One gain does appear, at top-1: under plain beam search, blocking copy reads at every digit raises RQ-KMeans 3×128's new-target HR@1 by +0.6 pp [+0.1, +1.1]. With copying off, the top slot sometimes goes to a correct new item; within the top-10 the loss outweighs it.

5 · Next-two · exp5

The second item is built from the first

In the next-two model, the second recommended item depends on the first much more than on the user's most recent item. It reuses the same prefix-matched copying: when item 1 already shares item 2's first digits, changing item 1's later digits costs item 2 0.43 to 0.73 nats, and about zero otherwise. Item 1's information reaches item 2 in the same late window, layers 20 to 26.

6 · Mechanism, DiffGRM · exp7, exp8

DiffGRM copies too, through its encoder

DiffGRM shows the same prefix-matched copying. But blocking cross-attention from the digit position to the matching item's slot changes nothing, in any decoder block, from any digit. DiffGRM encodes each history item as one token, and its single encoder layer mixes the items. So exp8 blocked the encoder route as well: no other history slot may read the item. The encoder route alone carries much of the copy. With both routes blocked, about two thirds of it is gone. The direct read is a backup, and it only matters once the spread is blocked.

What we learned about the evaluation

Reproducing the archived AR predictions exposed four things about how the reported numbers were produced.

  • The archived AR decoder was beam sampling. evaluate.py passes a GenerationConfig without sampling settings, so transformers 4.57.1 merges the checkpoint's Qwen-Instruct defaults into it: sampling on, temperature 0.7, top-k 20, top-p 0.8, repetition penalty 1.1. The sweep ran on that version. Each beam is cut to a small nucleus, and the repetition penalty works against copying history tokens. The archive reproduces exactly under these settings (3,680 of 3,681 rows with identical 50-SID lists). Plain beam search, with no sampling and no repetition penalty, gives HR@10 24.7% and 15.3%, against 24.0% and 14.4% for the archived decoder.
  • Turning the sampling off takes more than setting it. From transformers 4.50, generate() refills every field left at its library default from the checkpoint's generation_config.json. do_sample=False and repetition_penalty=1.0 are those defaults, so passing them changes nothing; only use_model_defaults=False gives plain beam search. exp6's first plain-decoder run fell into this trap and was re-run (protocol amendment 2).
  • The frozen RQ-VAE AR checkpoint is not the archived model. oneoff_rqvae4cb128 reproduces 0 of 3,681 archived RQ-VAE lists, and its HR@10 under the archive's own rule is 14.5% against the recorded 13.1%. It comes from an earlier training run with the same configuration, so RQ-VAE AR results describe the one-off.
  • The prompt wording changed upstream. OneDiffRec commit a165f65 (2026-09-13) stripped trailing spaces from the prompt templates, which changes the token ids. The frozen checkpoints predate it, and SidLens rebuilds their exact prompts. Any checkpoint trained after that date needs the new wording.
  • Duplicate review records inflate HR@10. Some reviews appear twice in the dataset, as the same item on the same day. When one copy is the target, the model only has to copy the other. These rows are a few percent of each split but a sixth of the RQ-VAE model's hits (section 9).
9 · Time · retrospective exp6, exp9

In the data, "recent" means the same day

No model is given a timestamp. The AR prompt lists the history "in chronological order", and DiffGRM has a recency position, so both see only order. The frozen reviews keep a day for every event, and sidlens.data.timestamps puts it back on all 43,102 of them. That exposes three facts about the data. Reviews come in same-day bursts. Within a day, the order is ASIN order, because upstream breaks ties by raw-file order. And some reviews are recorded twice.

Split by time, the data say something simple. Whether a history item shares the target's first digit depends on how long before the target it was reviewed, and hardly at all on where it sits in the list. A same-day item seven places back is about as informative as the most recent one; an item from over a year ago is uninformative wherever it sits.

The models cannot see this. RQ-KMeans AR copies the most recent item's first digit in 67% of same-day rows and in 67% of rows where that item is over a year old. The data justify the copy in 33% and 3% of them. The other three models follow a quarter to two fifths of the drop, which can only come from content cues.

exp9 tests order directly. It swaps the two most recent items and keeps everything else fixed. When both were reviewed on the same day and the target came later, the swap leaves every timestamp as it was, and the data treat the two items as exchangeable. RQ-KMeans still prefers whichever item is last, by 0.36 on those rows against 0.38 where the last item really is later (0.35 on the valid split). RQ-VAE is only weakly positional (0.07 and 0.10), and it follows the truly later item about as strongly (0.07).

So an arbitrary order moves real decisions. On tied rows the swap flips 50% of RQ-KMeans' first-digit choices and changes its top-1 recommended SID in 42%, though HR@10 stays level. Averaging the model over the two orders the timestamps cannot tell apart gains +0.027 nats on those rows. It is a free, timestamp-aware inference rule, and it helps only where it is licensed.

Time also changes how section 4 reads. The benefit of copying sits almost entirely on rows whose target falls on the most recent item's day. It does nothing for a new item reviewed on a later day, the largest kind of target. Part of the benefit is an artefact: duplicate review records are 2.7% of test rows, but they supply 11% (RQ-KMeans) and 17% (RQ-VAE) of all HR@10 hits. Duplicates also explain part of the gap between valid and test effect sizes (section 12). Valid has twice test's share of them, and reweighting test to valid's mix moves the exp4 ratio from 1.51 to 1.25 (RQ-KMeans) and from 1.65 to 1.32 (RQ-VAE).

Associational where it re-reads earlier outputs; exp9 is the controlled test of order. Review days are not purchase days. The DiffGRM cohort (leave-last-out) is not a time split: its targets span 2013 to 2018.

10 · Features · representation exp2

What sparse autoencoders find inside the AR models

The probes in section 3 read the model through questions we chose in advance. A sparse autoencoder (SAE) instead learns its own basis: each activation is rewritten as a sum of a few directions, called latents, out of 12,288. We fitted one SAE per model at layers 12, 16, 20 and 24, on train-split activations, and read the test split in that basis. A latent can then be removed from the real activation without touching the SAE's reconstruction error, which makes it a unit for a causal test.

Four things come out.

  • Copy features exist and are causal, late. A handful of code-specific latents at the first-digit readout fire on the recent item's first digit. Removing them costs the copy most at layers 20 and 24, where exp3's patching and the logit lens put the copy decision. They carry little of the readout's activation, though. Earlier, the digit is decodable (section 3) but not held in features whose removal matters.
  • The model detects the prefix match, but the output does not read it there. RQ-KMeans has sharp latents that tell whether the recent item matches the prefix so far. Removing them at the readout changes nothing. With exp4's single-edge result, this points to the match being computed inside attention's query–key comparison. That narrows the open question of where the match is computed.
  • Nothing about the target beyond the history. Neither the residual nor the latents predict the target item's content embedding better than a linear map of the history's items.
  • The model does sense same-day bursts. Its readout predicts whether the most recent item was reviewed on the target's day better than five SID-overlap features do. RQ-KMeans' copying ignores that signal (section 9).

One SAE per model and layer, fitted on about 0.5M tokens with nothing tuned. The dead-latent share is high in RQ-KMeans' early layers. An ablation at one layer and one position leaves later layers free to re-read the history, so a null effect bounds that position's role, not the whole computation.

12 · Limits

Limits and next experiments

  • Scores. Teacher-forced (AR) and fixed-state (DiffGRM) scores are conditional log-probabilities. Only exp6 measures hit rate.
  • Training seeds. Each checkpoint was trained once, so the intervals are conditional on these weights.
  • Controls. Replacement items are uniform catalogue items, not category-matched, because category labels are not on this cluster.
  • Knockouts. A knockout removes direct reads; indirect routes are bounded (exp4, exp8), not eliminated.
  • Post-hoc hypothesis. Prefix-matched copying was found after the fact in exp3, then confirmed on a held-out split (exp4) and in the other model family (exp7). exp8 was designed after seeing exp7.
  • Split sizes. AR effects on the valid split are 1.5 to 1.65 times the test-split ones. About half of that is the periods' mix of duplicate-record and same-day rows (section 9); the rest is unexplained.
  • Time. Review days are not purchase days, and the time strata also differ in content (same-day items are often bundles). exp9 controls order; the rest of section 9 is associational.

Next experiments

  • DiffGRM decoding: does copying help or hurt its hit rate? This is exp6 for DiffGRM, blocking both routes inside the validated beam search.
  • Where the prefix match is computed: section 10 rules out a readout feature for RQ-KMeans. Next is the attention query–key match itself in layers 14 to 27, with SAEs on attention inputs.
  • Whether the burst signal can steer copying: push the readout along the same-day direction and see whether the copy responds.
  • Report HR with duplicate-record targets excluded or flagged, for the paper's AR and DiffGRM numbers.
  • Category-matched controls and category probes, once the Industrial labels are pulled onto this cluster.
  • A plain-beam re-evaluation of the AR sweep numbers for the paper.
