DiffGRM Mechanisms Report

SidLens · DiffGRM next-item models on Amazon Industrial & Scientific (6,297 test users, 20 checkpoints) · built 2026-10-15

Summary

How does DiffGRM, a masked-diffusion recommender over semantic IDs, arrive at a recommendation? Four new experiments ran on all 20 next-item checkpoints, so every result can be compared across SID digits, depths, quantizers and codebook widths:

  • linear probes, sparse autoencoders and a description autoencoder on the encoder;
  • activation patching between paired runs;
  • a step-level history switch;
  • three perturbations held through beam search.
  • The encoder rewrites every history slot as recent context. After its one layer a slot's own first code is only 0.31–0.53 linearly readable (input: 0.98–1.00), while the most recent item becomes readable from every other slot (+0.15–0.41). The encoder holds no information about new targets beyond the history's content. When each slot is rebuilt from an eight-word description, the decoder needs the words about the recent item, not the slot's own.
  • The decoder finds the recent item in the other slots, and each digit reads it through its own cross-attention. Clean states of the other slots restore 0.83–0.90 of a replaced r1's effect, and r1's own slot 0.32–0.53. The read is spread over all four blocks.
  • Digits talk in the first decoder block. A revealed digit's effect on the next digit has moved to the predicted position after block 1. The direct self-attention edge there carries 0.37–0.89 of it, less at depth 5.
  • The first reveal step decides most, and wider codebooks decide earlier. Replacing r1 at the first step only gives the replaced-history recommendation for 0.16–0.39 of affected users at width 128 and 0.25–0.68 at width 512. No single step decides alone.
  • Copying helps only re-purchases. Blocking every route to r1 through the whole beam search costs 1.0–3.7 points of SID HR@10 against a control: 12–39 points on targets that are r1's own item, -0.7 to +0.4 on new targets. That is the AR pattern.
  • DiffGRM tracks items, not slots, but its positions are absolute. Swapping r1 and r2 rarely moves the copied code to the new last slot. Shifting the whole history by five slots changes the top-1 for 0.47–0.82 of users.

11 of 20 pre-registered expectations held. The failures are findings and are listed under Pre-registered expectations. Every run passed its bit-exact controls. One exp14 cell failed its archive gate and is excluded.

What was run

DiffGRM is a masked (absorbing) discrete diffusion recommender. A one-layer encoder turns each history item (its SID digits through item_mlp, plus a slot position) into one token. A four-block decoder holds one position per SID digit, each either a revealed code or a mask. Its self-attention runs over the digits and its cross-attention reads the history slots. A beam search reveals one (position, code) per step. Between steps only the partial SID passes on. The questions here are what the encoder holds, where in the network a history change or a revealed digit has its effect, at which reveal step the history matters, and whether the copying found earlier helps the lists.

What was already known

Five earlier results frame the new ones. Controlled exp1 and exp2 showed that the reveal order matters, and that the confidence policy loses most against fixed orders at depth 5 and beam 64. The reveal trace (representation/exp4, 16 of 20 cells, provisional) showed that scoring the catalogue once from the fully masked state falls short of the beam, and that most depth-5 decode states were never visited in training. exp7 and exp8 (two matched cells) showed that DiffGRM leans on the most recent item r1 and copies prefix-matched items as the AR models do. They also showed that the copy reaches the decoder through the encoder, not through a direct read of r1's slot.

X1 · What the encoder holds (probes, SAEs, description autoencoder)

The encoder has one block. It turns each history item (its SID digits through item_mlp, plus a slot position) into one state, and the decoder reads only these states. Three tools read them on all 20 checkpoints: linear probes at the block input, after self-attention and at the output; TopK sparse autoencoders on the output, whose latents are then ablated; and a description autoencoder. The last is a scaled-down natural language autoencoder: a verbalizer writes each state as eight interpretable words (recency, category path, store, price, first code, and what the most recent item looks like), and a reconstructor rebuilds the state from those words.

  • One encoder layer turns each slot from "this item" into "this user's recent context". A slot's own first code is linearly readable at the input almost perfectly (0.98–1.00); at the output, the only thing the decoder reads, 0.31–0.53. Its category falls from 0.86–0.98 to 0.37–0.50. Over the same layer the slot gains its recency (+0.20–0.31 accuracy) and the most recent item's first code, read from the other slots (+0.15–0.41, interval above 0 in every cell).
  • Nothing about the target beyond the history's content. On users whose target is new, the encoder output predicts the target's first code no better than the input does (output − input between -0.011 and +0.010). This is the AR models' result.
  • SAEs reconstruct the state; few latents are single named attributes. The splice keeps 0.87–0.99 of the decoder's behaviour. 0.90–0.95 of live latents match no single attribute. The named ones encode own category, recency, or r1's first code in other slots; almost none encode the slot's own SID digits.
  • Spread latents are used. Ablating the active latents that encode r1's first code in other slots lowers the copy score by up to 0.36 nats more than a matched control (19 of 20 cells with the interval above 0). They are active for a minority of users, so they are part of the encoder route, not all.
  • Eight words explain about half of a state, and the decoder needs the words about the recent item. Rebuilding every slot from its description keeps 0.39–0.85 of the decoder's behaviour (FVU 0.44–0.76). The verbalizer's own words beat the true labels. Dropping r1's category is the most damaging cut in 17 cells, and r1's first code in 3. Dropping the slot's own words costs little.

X2 · Paired-run activation patching

Two runs of one checkpoint differ in one input. Part H replaces the most recent item r1 with exp7's control. Part S keeps the history and gives the revealed parent digit a sibling's code. A piece of one run is patched into the other, and the share of the scored digit's KL that comes back is read off.

  • The replaced item reaches the decoder through the other history slots. Restoring every other slot's clean encoder state recovers 0.83–0.90 of the first-digit effect (copy rows: 0.83–0.96). Restoring r1's own slot recovers 0.32–0.53, and the second most recent slot alone recovers about the same (0.36–0.52). Noising agrees: the replaced history in the other slots reproduces 0.47–0.68 of the damage, in r1's own slot only 0.10–0.17. This holds in all 20 cells, every quantizer, depth and width.
  • Each digit reads the history through its own cross-attention, redundantly across blocks. The best single block's cross-attention output at the predicted digit restores 0.34–0.77 of the effect, and the four blocks overlap. The same patch at another digit position restores at most 0.23 in block 1 and about 0 after it (at most 0.07).
  • A revealed digit reaches the digit being predicted in the first decoder block, mostly over the direct edge. The predicted position's residual after block 1 restores 0.65–0.96 of the effect of changing the parent digit (after block 2: 0.91–0.99). Cutting the self-attention edge from the predicted digit to the parent removes 0.37–0.89 of the effect, almost all of it in block 1 (0.36–0.88). The share falls with depth: 0.68–0.89 at depth 3, 0.37–0.66 at depth 5.

X3 · Which reveal step uses the history

Between reveal steps only the partial SID passes on, so a step depends on the history only through its own forward. Each step can therefore be given another history: r1 replaced by exp7's control (H′) at some steps, the real history at the rest. The question is how much of the final recommendation one step decides.

  • The first step decides most, but no step decides alone. Among users whose recommendation changes when r1 is replaced, using H′ at the first step only gives the replaced-history top-1 for 0.16–0.68 of them (beam 64), and at the last step only for 0.01–0.10. Summed over steps this is well below 1: the history is used throughout the decode.
  • Wider codebooks commit earlier. The first-step share is larger at width 512 than at 128 in 9 of 9 quantizer × depth pairs (0.25–0.68 vs 0.16–0.39). The pre-registered expectation that depth-5 models (never trained on the fully masked state) would commit less at step 1 failed: depth matters less than width.
  • Later steps still steer. Switching one step changes the final top-1 for 0.69–0.93 of these users at the first step and 0.04–0.34 at the last. In greedy decoding, a change caused by one step is most often a change of which digit is revealed at that step (0.38–0.87 at the first step), not only of its code.
  • Replacing r1 costs hit rate: SID HR@10 falls from 5.3–20.5 % to 2.9–14.7 % when every step uses H′.

X4 · Perturbations held through decoding

These interventions are held for the whole beam search (beam 256), and the effect is read on the recommendation lists. Part B blocks every route to r1, the most recent item: no other encoder slot may read it, and no decoder position may read its slot, at any step. This is the DiffGRM counterpart of the AR copy knockout (exp6). The control blocks the same routes to an older item with a different first code. Part O swaps r1 and r2, or moves the whole history one or five slots to the right.

  • Copying the recent item helps, and only for items the user already had. Blocking r1 lowers SID HR@10 by 1.0–3.7 points more than the control block (control alone: -0.4 to +0.5); at item level the drop is 1.0–2.9 points. The loss sits on targets that are r1's own item (4 % of users: 12–39 points), share r1's SID (0–15) or r1's first code (4–20). New targets (66–89 % of users) change by -0.7 to +0.4 points. This is the AR models' pattern (exp6, retrospective exp7).
  • DiffGRM follows the item, not the slot. When r1 and r2 swap places, users whose top-1 first code copied r1 keep r1's code 0.39–0.86 of the time and switch to the item now in the most recent slot only 0.00–0.18 of the time (post hoc measure; the declared PO1 was mis-scaled). The AR RQ-KMeans model followed position (exp9).
  • Yet absolute positions matter a great deal. Moving the whole history five slots to the right, order intact, changes the top-1 SID for 0.47–0.82 of users (one slot: 0.33–0.69; the swap: 0.27–0.66) and costs 0.5–3.5 points of HR@10. The encoder reads slot positions as absolute. Training always started histories at slot 1, so part of this is the novelty of leading padding.

19 cells passed the archive gate. MQ 5×512 failed it: at beam 256 one of 6,297 users lost a path at an intermediate beam cutoff, which the declared tie tolerance does not cover. That cell is excluded.

Limits and next steps

  • One checkpoint per cell. No interval covers training-seed variance; width and depth comparisons also differ by SID table (independent fits except the nested RQ-KMeans depths).
  • States. X1 and X2 score fixed reveal states (fully masked, golden prefix). X3 and X4 act on the decoding path itself.
  • The description autoencoder uses a vocabulary we chose and a supervised verbalizer. It finds only what was named. A full natural language autoencoder (LLM verbalizer and reconstructor trained by RL on reconstruction) is the next step if named attributes leave behaviour unexplained.
  • SAEs are fitted once per cell on training-target histories (about 90k slots). Census families are assigned by chance-corrected selectivity; a latent can be read as a family without being used. Only CS1 and CS2 test use.
  • Shifted histories (X4) put padding at the start, which training never did.
  • Deferred: the implicit-step probe and patch, dose–response attention scaling, negative guidance, next-two models.
